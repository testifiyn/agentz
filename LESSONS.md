## 2026-09-20 — Paid third-party investigation of a named individual for someone else's decision about them is structurally FCRA/GDPR-blocked at €0 capital

**Hypothesis:** A productized service where a solo operator investigates a
specific person (identity, background, behavior, references) and delivers
a risk judgment to a paying third party could be built and operated at €0
capital, because the moat is human judgment/reputation rather than
clonable code, sidestepping the "AI-clone-in-days" problem that kills
software-shaped candidates.

**What happened:** The specific candidate tested (a manual fraud-vetting
concierge for startups hiring remote workers, addressing the real,
well-evidenced 2026 DPRK-fake-employee fraud wave) survived discovery-
stage screening and adversarial clone-resistance pressure — the first
candidate across 4 discovery rounds to do so. It was then killed at
dedicated legal-risk validation: 15 U.S.C. §1681a defines a "Consumer
Reporting Agency" functionally, not by label — assembling/evaluating
information on an individual, used for an employment (or similar
eligibility) decision, by a third party for a fee. The FTC's 1999 Vail
advisory opinion establishes that even fact-finding/investigation work
(not just scored reports) counts, and Congress had to carve out a narrow
statutory exception (§1681a(y)) specifically for workplace-misconduct
investigations — general hiring vetting isn't covered by that exception.
The CFPB's Nov 2024 Circular 2024-06 directly addresses this fact
pattern. No "pure verification, not evaluation" reframe survives contact
with this precedent — fact-verification-only is itself the classic CRA
product line — and "we're advising the founder, not reporting on the
candidate" is exactly the kind of substance-over-form distinction FTC/
courts have already rejected. A compliant version is theoretically
buildable but requires legal counsel, dispute/accuracy infrastructure,
and insurance — real money and real risk this €0 project cannot absorb.
GDPR adds independent exposure for any EU/UK-based subject, which is
near-certain in a global remote-hiring candidate pool, so restricting to
non-US customers doesn't dodge it either.

**Why the assumption was wrong:** The clone-resistance theory (human
judgment/reputation as moat) is a real and useful lens for *competitive*
risk, but it's orthogonal to *regulatory* risk. A service can be
perfectly clone-resistant and still be illegal to operate without capital
this project doesn't have. The two risk classes must be checked
independently — surviving one says nothing about the other.

**Generalized implication — screen out at discovery stage, don't wait for
validation:** any candidate where the paid deliverable is a third party's
investigation, verification, or evaluation of a *specific named individual*,
sold to *someone else* who will use it to make a decision about that
person (hire/fire, rent/evict, date/avoid, lend/deny, insure/decline,
associate/reject), is presumptively FCRA-shaped (US) and/or GDPR-shaped
(EU/UK) regardless of framing, labeling, or whether the output is called
a "report," a "risk memo," "advisory," or "consulting." This rules out,
by the same logic, without needing individual re-validation: tenant
screening, contractor/gig-worker vetting, dating-safety background
checks, "verify my online seller/buyer" trust services, and similar
person-investigation-for-hire ideas — unless a specific legal-counsel
review (a real cost this project doesn't currently have budget for)
clears a narrowly scoped exception. Future discovery should redirect
service-model candidates toward value delivered about non-personal
subject matter (documents, markets, code, data, processes, businesses-
as-entities) rather than risk judgments about named individuals, or
toward services where the customer is investigating *themselves* /
their own material (which doesn't implicate third-party consumer-report
law the same way).

**Also carried forward from this same round (content/community axis):**
a distinct, unrelated finding — professional/hobbyist content-curation
niches are overwhelmingly already owned by (a) an established expert
newsletter with a 10-15+ year head start, (b) a >10k-member trade
association delivering the same curation as a membership benefit, or (c)
a vendor content-marketing swarm with no pressure to monetize the content
itself. This is a compressed-to-*years* incumbency problem, structurally
different from the compressed-to-*weeks* clone problem that kills
software-shaped candidates — screen future content-axis candidates for
both before investing research time.
